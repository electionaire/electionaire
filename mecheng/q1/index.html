Method

  1. Check for singularity first. Two equal or proportional rows (or columns), or \(\det A = 0\), make \(A\) singular and \(\operatorname{cond}(A)=\infty\).
  2. Singular: count solutions. \(Ax=b\) is solvable only if \(b\) obeys the same dependency as the rows. Consistent gives infinitely many solutions (add any null vector), inconsistent gives none.
  3. Nonsingular: exactly one solution. \(\operatorname{cond}_2(A)=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}\). For symmetric \(A\) this is \(|\lambda|_{\max}/|\lambda|_{\min}\) of the eigenvalues.
  4. Use block structure. A lone diagonal entry is itself an eigenvalue. Include it when you pick the largest and smallest.
  5. Triangular solves. \(LUx=b\): forward substitution \(Ly=b\) from the top, then backward substitution \(Ux=y\) from the bottom.
\(y_i=\dfrac{b_i-\sum_{j<i}L_{ij}y_j}{L_{ii}}\qquad x_i=\dfrac{y_i-\sum_{j>i}U_{ij}x_j}{U_{ii}}\)
Trap. In Nov 2024 the 2×2 block alone has eigenvalues 6 and 14, but the decoupled 1 is smaller, so \(\operatorname{cond}=14/1\), not \(14/6\).

Lab

Every exam matrix is block diagonal: \(x_1\) decouples, and the 2×2 block is two lines in the \((x_2,x_3)\) plane. Crossing lines: one solution. Parallel: none. The same line: infinitely many. Nearly parallel lines: a large condition number.
