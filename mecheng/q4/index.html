Method

  1. Turn the symbol into a limit. \(f=O(g)\) as \(h\to0\) means \(|f/g|\) stays bounded; \(f=o(g)\) means \(|f/g|\to0\). Every \(o\) is also an \(O\).
  2. Form the ratio and simplify. \(\frac{h\ln|h|}{h}=\ln|h|\to-\infty\): unbounded, so \(h\ln|h|\) is neither \(O(h)\) nor \(o(h)\).
  3. Logarithms lose to any power. \(h^{a}\ln|h|\to0\) for every \(a\gt0\). So \(h^{3/2}\ln|h|=h\cdot h^{1/2}\ln|h|=o(h)\), hence \(O(h)\).
  4. Use the algebra rules. \(O(h^p)\,O(h^q)=O(h^{p+q})\), \(O(h^p)+O(h^{p+1})=O(h^p)\), \(O(h^{p+2})/h^{p}=O(h^2)\).
  5. Observed order from data. Halving \(h\) divides the error by about \(2^p\), so \(p\approx\log_2\frac{e(h)}{e(h/2)}\). Trust the smallest \(h\).
  6. Points, derivative and order. An optimal \(q\)-point finite-difference formula for a \(k\)-th derivative has order \(p\) with \(k+p=q\).
Trap. A ratio that tends to a nonzero constant is \(O\) but not \(o\). Only a ratio that tends to zero is \(o\).
